A. INPUTS — MEASURED AND ASSUMED
==============================================================================

  Measured, for `llm_cited` (Phase 5 record):

    a  answers an answerable question      76 of 95    80.0%
    b  an answer is unsupported            3 of 76      3.9%   95% interval [1.4%, 11.0%]
    r  refuses an unanswerable question    25 of 25   100.0%   95% interval [86.7%, 100.0%]

  Assumed, and varied:

    k  cost of one bad answer, in deflected tickets   [1, 5, 10, 20, 50]
    p  share of traffic that is unanswerable          [0.05, 0.1, 0.21]
    f  cost of a refusal, in deflected tickets        0.1

  Value per query, against sending every ticket to a person (zero):

    (1 - p) * [ a * ((1 - b) - b*k) - (1 - a)*f ]  -  p * [ (1 - r)*k + r*f ]

  "Bad" means the judge found a claim the context does not support. The
  share of traffic that is unanswerable is NOT the golden set's 21%:
  that mix was designed, and is included only as the pessimistic end.

==============================================================================
B. WHERE THE SYSTEM STOPS PAYING
==============================================================================

  Ignore unanswerable questions entirely. Answering ANSWERABLE ones
  stops paying once a bad answer costs more than this many deflected
  tickets:

    at the measured bad-answer rate     3.9%     k = 23.7
    at the top of its interval         11.0%     k = 7.9
    at the bottom of its interval       1.4%     k = 71.1

  Past that point no refusal rate helps: the system loses on the
  questions it is supposed to be good at. So the first thing the
  business has to say is whether one wrong billing answer costs more or
  less than about 8 tickets' worth of saved effort — and 3 bad
  answers in 76 cannot place that threshold more precisely than
  somewhere between 8 and 71.

==============================================================================
C. THE REFUSAL RATE THE BUSINESS NEEDS
==============================================================================

  Refusal rate needed to break even, at the measured bad-answer rate (3.9%):

    bad answer costs          p = 5%     p = 10%     p = 21%
    --------------------------------------------------------
    k = 1                        any         any         any
    k = 5                        any         any       56.7%
    k = 10                     18.0%       61.7%       84.6%
    k = 20                   89.3% *     95.2% *     98.3% *
    k = 50                     never       never       never

  Refusal rate needed to break even, at the top of its interval (11.0%):

    bad answer costs          p = 5%     p = 10%     p = 21%
    --------------------------------------------------------
    k = 1                        any         any         any
    k = 5                       3.9%       55.5%       82.6%
    k = 10                     never       never       never
    k = 20                     never       never       never
    k = 50                     never       never       never

    any    pays at every refusal rate
    never  does not pay even with perfect refusal
    *      higher than the 86.7% that 25 of 25 has established

  Read down a column. In the base case (k = 10, p = 10%) the system
  needs to refuse 61.7% of unanswerable questions, and 25 of 25 already
  shows at least 86.7%. The sample the memo called too small is, for that
  case, enough. Where the table says "never", it is not the refusal
  rate that fails.

==============================================================================
D. WHICH UNCERTAINTY MOVES THE DECISION
==============================================================================

  Two rates are uncertain: the refusal rate (25 of 25) and the
  bad-answer rate (3 of 76). Moving each across its own 95% interval,
  holding the other at its measured value, in the base case:

    value per query at the measured rates              +0.379
    swing from the refusal rate   [86.7% to 100.0%]     0.132
    swing from the bad-answer rate [1.4% to 11.0%]     0.762

>>> The bad-answer rate moves the result 5.8x as much as the refusal
    rate does.

  The memo's headline caveat was the refusal sample. On this model that
  is the smaller of the two uncertainties. The larger one is how often
  an answer to an ANSWERABLE question contains an unsupported claim,
  and it is larger for a plain reason: at p = 10%, nine queries in ten
  are answerable, so an error rate on that side is multiplied by nine
  times the traffic.

  Both at their unfavourable ends at once, the base case is worth
  -0.309 per query; both favourable, +0.585.

  Does the decision survive the uncertainty? Value per query with both
  rates at their unfavourable ends, by assumption:

    bad answer costs          p = 5%     p = 10%     p = 21%
    --------------------------------------------------------
    k = 1                +0.56 holds +0.52 holds +0.43 holds
    k = 5                +0.20 holds +0.15 holds +0.04 holds
    k = 10                -0.25 open  -0.31 open  -0.44 open
    k = 20                -1.15 open  -1.23 open  -1.42 open
    k = 50               -3.85 fails -4.00 fails -4.34 fails

    holds  positive even at the unfavourable ends: deploy on this evidence
    open   positive at the measured rates, negative at the unfavourable
           ends: the evidence cannot decide, and more data would
    fails  negative at the measured rates: more data will not help

  Of 15 combinations, 6 hold, 6 are open and 3 fail. The pilot is
  only worth running for the open ones — and what it must tighten
  there is mostly the bad-answer rate.

==============================================================================
E. WHAT THE PILOT HAS TO MEASURE
==============================================================================

  1. THE BAD-ANSWER RATE. In the base case the system breaks even while
     no more than 7.1% of its answers are unsupported. The measured
     rate is 3.9% with an upper bound of 11.0%. To bring the upper bound
     under 7.1%, if the true rate is what was measured, the pilot needs
     agents to review about 273 drafted answers.

     And a rule for stopping early: 13 or more bad drafts in the first
     100 puts even the lower end of the interval above 7.1%. At that
     point more data cannot rescue the base case, and the pilot ends.

  2. THE REFUSAL RATE. The base case needs 61.7%. A clean run of
     7 unanswerable tickets establishes that; 25 already have.
     Nothing further is needed here unless a bad answer costs far more
     than assumed.

  3. WHETHER IT HELPS AT ALL. Neither rate says the assistant improves
     support. That needs a controlled comparison: tickets handled with
     a drafted answer against tickets handled without. To detect a lift
     in first-contact resolution from 70% to 73% (two-sided, 5%
     significance, 80% power) takes 3,551 tickets per arm if tickets are
     randomised individually.

     Randomising by AGENT is cleaner, because an agent who has seen
     drafts cannot unsee them. It costs precision: at 50 tickets per
     agent and an intra-agent correlation of 0.05, the design effect is
     3.45, so 12,251 tickets per arm — 24,502 in total, about
     25 days at 1,000 tickets a day.

  The resolution baseline, the smallest lift worth detecting, the
  correlation and the ticket volume are placeholders. They are inputs
  to replace with the support team's figures, and the notebook
  recomputes everything when they change.
